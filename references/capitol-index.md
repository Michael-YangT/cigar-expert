## Anatomy — 36 terms

### Band (Anilla)
` /ah-NEE-yah/ | Anatomy` — `band`
Paper ring around the cigar carrying the brand, normally placed near the head. Gustave Bock is usually credited with popularizing the band in the nineteenth century; its practical purposes were identifying brands and keeping gloves clean.

### Barrel
`Anatomy` — `barrel`
Straight-sided body of the cigar between head and foot. Spec sheets sometimes use body or shank for the same section, and it is the part that carries the band and shows the wrapper seams.

### Binder (Capote)
` /kah-POH-teh/ | Anatomy` — `binder`
Leaf wrapped around the bunch to hold the filler together and give the cigar its shape before the wrapper goes on. Binders are chosen for tensile strength and combustion rather than beauty, and often come from thicker primings or from leaves rejected as wrapper.

### Bunch
`Anatomy` — `bunch`
Filler leaves gathered and folded, then enclosed in the binder, forming the inner cigar before the wrapper goes on. The bunch establishes draw, burn rate, and most of how the blend actually behaves.

### Cap
`Anatomy` — `cap`
Piece of wrapper leaf applied over the head to seal the end where the wrapper spiral terminates. Caps are cut from wrapper offcuts and fixed with a trace of vegetable gum; on a well-made cigar the join is nearly invisible.

### Cellophane
`Anatomy` — `cellophane`
Clear sleeve fitted over individual cigars in many non-Cuban boxes. Made from wood pulp, it is slightly permeable, so cigars continue to breathe and age inside it, though more slowly than naked leaf.

### Closed Foot
`Anatomy` — `closed-foot`
Foot covered by a small piece of wrapper so no filler is exposed, a Cuban tradition also called a perfecto foot or pie cerrado. Lighting one means burning through wrapper leaf first.

### Cold Draw
`Anatomy` — `cold-draw`
Pull of air taken through the unlit cigar after cutting, also called the dry draw. It reveals both the mechanical resistance of the bunch and the raw, unburned flavors of the filler and wrapper.

### Filler (Tripa)
` /TREE-pah/ | Anatomy` — `filler`
Core of the cigar: the leaves bunched inside the binder that supply most of the tobacco volume and the backbone of the blend. Filler is normally a mix of leaf classes and origins balanced for strength, aroma, and combustion.

### Flag Cap
`Anatomy` — `flag-cap`
Cap formed by leaving a tail of wrapper at the head and folding or twisting it over the top instead of applying a separate round piece. The flag can be laid flat, swirled into a spiral, or left standing as a pigtail.

### Foot
`Anatomy` — `foot`
End that gets lit, where the filler is exposed in cross section on a standard parejo. Closed and shaggy feet are deliberate variations on the open foot.

### Foot Band
`Anatomy` — `foot-band`
Band applied at the foot rather than the head, sometimes wrapping over exposed filler. Used for anniversary marks, blend information, or to dress a closed foot.

### Head
`Anatomy` — `head`
End that goes in the mouth, finished with a cap and normally the end nearest the band. Also called the mouth end; on a figurado the head is tapered, pointed, or flattened rather than round.

### Length
`Anatomy` — `length`
Distance from foot to head, quoted in inches in the United States and in millimetres in Cuba and most of Europe. Premium cigars run roughly 4 to 9 inches, with 5 to 7 covering the bulk of production.

### Ligero
` /lee-HEH-roh/ | Anatomy` — `ligero`
Filler leaf from the top of the plant, thick, oily, and slow burning after long fermentation. Ligero carries the most nicotine and the most concentrated flavor, and is normally placed at the centre of the bunch.

### Long Filler
`Anatomy` — `long-filler`
Filler made of whole leaves that run the full length of the cigar, folded rather than chopped. Long filler is the standard for premium handmade cigars and is what the term hecho a mano implies in practice.

### Medio Tiempo
` /MEH-dyoh TYEM-poh/ | Anatomy` — `medio-tiempo`
Two small leaves that sometimes grow at the very crown of a tobacco plant, above the ligero primings, and appear on only a minority of plants. Cohiba Behike is the best known cigar to advertise medio tiempo in its blend.

### Mixed Filler
`Anatomy` — `mixed-filler`
Blend of long-filler leaf and short filler in the same cigar, also sold as medium filler or a Cuban sandwich. Long leaf provides structure and draw while the scrap fills volume at lower cost.

### Oils
`Anatomy` — `oils`
Visible sheen on a wrapper produced by resins and essential oils that survive fermentation and remain in the leaf. Oily wrappers look lacquered under direct light and generally indicate correct humidification and unhurried curing.

### Pigtail
`Anatomy` — `pigtail`
Twisted tail of wrapper left protruding from the head, also called a curly head. Traditional on Cuban culebras and lanceros, it is decorative and can be snapped off with the fingers rather than cut.

### Priming
`Anatomy` — `priming`
Horizontal level of leaves on a tobacco plant, harvested from the bottom upward over several weeks. Cuban practice names the levels from the ground up as libre de pie, uno y medio, centro ligero, centro fino, centro gordo, and corona; higher primings take more sun and yield thicker, stronger leaf.

### Ring Gauge
`Anatomy` — `ring-gauge`
Diameter of a cigar measured in 64ths of an inch, so a 50 ring gauge is 50/64 of an inch, just under 20 mm. Cuban catalogues list diameter in millimetres and convert to ring gauge for export markets.

### Seam
`Anatomy` — `seam`
Line where each turn of the wrapper overlaps the previous one as it spirals up the cigar. Tight, flat, nearly invisible seams indicate careful wrapper application on properly humidified leaf.

### Seco
` /SEH-koh/ | Anatomy` — `seco`
Middle-priming filler leaf, lighter in body than ligero and quicker to burn. Seco supplies aroma and mid-palate flavor and is usually the largest share of a filler blend by volume.

### Secondary Band
`Anatomy` — `secondary-band`
Additional band placed below the main band to mark a line extension, limited edition, or vintage. Cuban Ediciones Limitadas carry a black and silver secondary band; many non-Cuban brands use one to distinguish blends within a marca.

### Shaggy Foot
`Anatomy` — `shaggy-foot`
Foot where the filler leaves are left protruding past the wrapper and binder, giving a frayed, tasseled appearance. It is a deliberate finish, not a fault.

### Shank
`Anatomy` — `shank`
Straight section of a cigar's body, used most often for the untapered portion of a figurado between the head taper and the foot. Usage varies by factory, and some specification sheets treat shank simply as a synonym for barrel.

### Short Filler
`Anatomy` — `short-filler`
Chopped or scrap tobacco used as filler, often the trimmings left over from cutting long-filler cigars. Also called picadura, it burns faster and hotter than whole leaf and is typical of machine-made cigars and inexpensive bundles.

### Shoulder
`Anatomy` — `shoulder`
Curved transition between the flat top of the head and the straight side of the barrel, where the edge of the cap sits. On most parejos the shoulder is a few millimetres below the top of the cap.

### Tooth
`Anatomy` — `tooth`
Fine bumpy texture on a wrapper, visible as small raised dots across the leaf. Tooth comes from oil glands and cell structure and is most pronounced on sun-grown wrappers such as Ecuadorian and Nicaraguan habano and on Cameroon leaf.

### Triple Cap
`Anatomy` — `triple-cap`
Cuban-style finish built from three separate pieces of wrapper laid over the head, leaving two visible seam lines plus the cap edge. Many non-Cuban factories imitate the look; some genuinely use three pieces, others build the same appearance from a folded flag.

### Tubo
` /TOO-boh/ | Anatomy` — `tubo`
Individual tube, usually aluminium, glass, or cedar-lined metal, holding a single cigar. Cuban tubos are typically lined with cedar; most designs slow humidity exchange but are not airtight.

### Vein
`Anatomy` — `vein`
Raised rib of leaf structure running through the wrapper. Prominent veins burn at a different rate than the surrounding lamina and are cosmetically undesirable, which is why wrapper is de-stemmed and graded for fine veining.

### Volado
` /voh-LAH-doh/ | Anatomy` — `volado`
Filler leaf from the lowest primings, thin and mild with modest flavor but excellent combustion. Volado is included chiefly to make the bunch burn evenly.

### Wrapper (Capa)
` /KAH-pah/ | Anatomy` — `wrapper`
Outermost leaf of the cigar, wound in a spiral from foot to head. Wrapper is the most expensive and most cosmetically demanding component, grown under shade or in full sun and selected for elasticity, fine veining, and even color. Estimates of its flavor contribution vary widely, but most makers put it well above its roughly 5 percent share of the cigar's weight.

### Wrapper Grade
`Anatomy` — `wrapper-grade`
Classification of wrapper leaf by size, color, texture, and freedom from damage, assigned at the escogida and refined at the rezagado bench. Systems differ by country and factory: shade-grown Connecticut is sorted into numbered grades, while Cuban wrapper is sorted into named color and size classes before it reaches a roller.

## Flavor & Tasting — 20 terms

### Ammonia
` /uh-MOHN-yuh/ | Flavor & Tasting` — `ammonia`
A sharp, acrid smell and taste from nitrogen compounds released during tobacco fermentation. Its presence in a finished cigar indicates the leaf has not fully rested.

### Balance
`Flavor & Tasting` — `balance`
The degree to which a cigar's components support rather than dominate one another, so that no single note, nor the strength, overruns the rest.

### Black Pepper
`Flavor & Tasting` — `black-pepper`
A tasting descriptor for a sharp, prickling spice, felt as much as tasted, and usually most pronounced on the retrohale. Frequently attributed to ligero and to Nicaraguan tobacco.

### Blind Tasting
`Flavor & Tasting` — `blind-tasting`
Evaluating cigars with the band removed or concealed, so that brand, price, and reputation cannot influence the assessment.

### Body
`Flavor & Tasting` — `body`
The weight, density, and mouthfeel of the smoke, described on a scale from light to full. Body is a textural judgment, independent of how much nicotine a cigar delivers.

### Cedar
`Flavor & Tasting` — `cedar`
A tasting descriptor for the aromatic, slightly sweet, resinous woodiness associated with Spanish cedar. It can come from the tobacco itself or be absorbed from box and humidor lining.

### Cocoa
` /KOH-koh/ | Flavor & Tasting` — `cocoa`
A tasting descriptor for unsweetened chocolate or cocoa powder, dry rather than confectionary. A frequent note in Ecuadorian and Mexican wrappers and in many maduro blends.

### Coffee
`Flavor & Tasting` — `coffee`
A tasting descriptor covering roasted coffee bean and espresso character, sometimes with a bitter edge. Common in darker, oilier wrappers.

### Complexity
`Flavor & Tasting` — `complexity`
The number and interplay of distinct flavors a cigar presents, and how they change over the course of the smoke. A complex cigar rewards attention; a simple one does not require it.

### Cream
`Flavor & Tasting` — `cream`
A tasting descriptor for a smooth, rounded, dairy-like texture and flavor, often reported alongside nuts or bread. Associated with Connecticut shade and other lighter wrappers.

### Earth
`Flavor & Tasting` — `earth`
A tasting descriptor for mineral, soil, mushroom, or forest floor character. Common in Cuban tobacco and in many Nicaraguan blends.

### Finish
`Flavor & Tasting` — `finish`
The flavors that remain on the palate after the smoke is exhaled, and how long they persist. Described by both length and character.

### Leather
`Flavor & Tasting` — `leather`
A tasting descriptor for a supple, tanned, faintly sweet character, sometimes further specified as saddle leather or new leather. Often paired with earth and cocoa.

### Nicotine Sickness
` /NIK-uh-teen/ | Flavor & Tasting` — `nicotine-sickness`
The acute effects of absorbing more nicotine than the smoker is accustomed to: nausea, sweating, dizziness, headache, and a racing pulse. Colloquially called being nicked or getting sick.

### Palate Fatigue
` /PAL-it/ | Flavor & Tasting` — `palate-fatigue`
The dulling of taste and smell perception over a long smoke or across several cigars, as receptors adapt to sustained stimulus.

### Retrohale
` /REH-troh-hale/ | Flavor & Tasting` — `retrohale`
Exhaling a portion of smoke through the nose so it passes over the olfactory receptors. Most of what people call taste is smell, so this reveals aromas the tongue cannot register.

### Scoring Methodology
`Flavor & Tasting` — `scoring-methodology`
The system by which a publication or taster converts an evaluation into a number. Most major outlets use a 100-point scale weighting appearance and construction, flavor, smoking characteristics, and overall impression, with the scale in practice compressed into the 80s and 90s.

### Strength
`Flavor & Tasting` — `strength`
The physiological impact of a cigar, driven chiefly by nicotine content in the filler. Rated light, medium, or full, and felt rather than tasted.

### Transition
`Flavor & Tasting` — `transition`
A shift in a cigar's flavor profile as it burns, whether gradual or abrupt. Reviewers typically map transitions against the thirds.

### Vertical Tasting
`Flavor & Tasting` — `vertical-tasting`
Smoking the same cigar from several different production years, or the same blend across several vitolas, in one sitting to isolate a single variable.

## Growing & Terroir — 33 terms

### Andullo
` /an-DOO-yo/ | Growing & Terroir` — `andullo`
A Dominican tradition in which cured leaves are compressed into a dense rope or log, bound and wrapped in royal palm bark, and left to ferment slowly for months or years. Andullo is traditionally shaved off for pipe or chew rather than rolled into cigars.

### Black Shank
`Growing & Terroir` — `black-shank`
A soilborne disease caused by the water mold Phytophthora nicotianae that rots the stalk base and roots, blackening the stem and wilting the plant. It persists in soil for years and spreads with standing water.

### Blue Mold (Moho Azul)
` /MO-o ah-SOOL/ | Growing & Terroir` — `blue-mold`
A fast-moving downy mildew caused by Peronospora tabacina, which forms bluish gray spores on leaf undersides and can strip a field within days in cool, damp weather. A devastating outbreak hit Cuba around 1979 and 1980.

### Cibao Valley
` /see-BOW/ | Growing & Terroir` — `cibao-valley`
The broad fertile valley in the northern Dominican Republic that runs through Santiago and Villa Gonzalez, and the country's main tobacco region. Piloto Cubano, Olor, and San Vicente all grow here, and Santiago holds most Dominican factories.

### Condega
` /kon-DEH-ga/ | Growing & Terroir` — `condega`
A Nicaraguan growing region between Esteli and Jalapa, in the department of Esteli. Its leaf tends toward the middle ground, with more body than Jalapa and more sweetness and elegance than Esteli.

### Connecticut River Valley
`Growing & Terroir` — `connecticut-river-valley`
The tobacco district straddling the Connecticut and Massachusetts stretch of the river, with sandy loam left by glacial floodplain. It produces two very different crops: shade-grown Connecticut wrapper under cheesecloth and sun-grown Broadleaf.

### Conuco
` /koh-NOO-koh/ | Growing & Terroir` — `conuco`
The Taino method of raised-mound agriculture, in which tobacco was cultivated alongside cassava, maize and sweet potatoes on mounds of soil built to prevent erosion and preserve fertility.

### Corojo Field Practice
` /ko-ROH-ho/ | Growing & Terroir` — `corojo-field-practice`
The intensive regime historically used for Cuban Corojo wrapper: shade cloth, tight plant spacing, heavy fungicide programs, careful topping, and picking two leaves at a time with cotton-gloved hands to avoid bruising. Leaves were sewn in pairs and hung immediately.

### Cosecha
` /ko-SEH-cha/ | Growing & Terroir` — `cosecha`
The harvest, and by extension the crop year. In the northern-hemisphere cigar belt, seed goes into beds in late summer or autumn, plants are transplanted in the dry season, and picking runs from roughly January into April.

### Cuban Denomination of Origin
`Growing & Terroir` — `cuban-denomination-of-origin`
The protected designation system that reserves terms such as Habano and Vuelta Abajo for tobacco grown and cigars made in defined Cuban zones under specified methods. Habanos S.A. administers the marks, which are recognized in most markets outside the United States.

### Esteli
` /es-teh-LEE/ | Growing & Terroir` — `esteli`
Nicaragua's principal tobacco city and growing region, written Esteli with an accent on the final syllable. Its dark, mineral-rich volcanic soil produces the country's strongest and most peppery leaf, and the city itself holds most of Nicaragua's major factories.

### Finca
` /FEEN-ka/ | Growing & Terroir` — `finca`
A farm or rural estate. In cigar country a finca usually includes fields, curing barns, and worker housing under one ownership, and premium producers often name their fincas on packaging.

### Harvest Passes
`Growing & Terroir` — `harvest-passes`
The sequence of trips through a field in which pickers take two or three leaves per plant, starting at the bottom and working up as each priming ripens. A crop typically requires five to eight passes spread over a month or more.

### Jalapa
` /ha-LAH-pa/ | Growing & Terroir` — `jalapa`
A valley in Nicaragua's Nueva Segovia department, near the Honduran border, with reddish sandy soil and higher elevation than Esteli. Its tobacco is the sweetest and most floral of Nicaragua's main zones.

### Jamastran
` /hah-mas-TRAHN/ | Growing & Terroir` — `jamastran`
A valley in the El Paraiso department of southeastern Honduras, near Danli, with hot conditions and rich soil. Jamastran produces some of Honduras's strongest and most full-flavored filler.

### Los Rios / Quevedo
` /los REE-os / keh-VEH-do/ | Growing & Terroir` — `los-rios-quevedo`
The Ecuadorian coastal province and town at the center of that country's wrapper industry. Persistent cloud cover off the Pacific filters sunlight for much of the growing season, giving shade-like conditions without cloth.

### Oriente
` /oh-ree-EN-teh/ | Growing & Terroir` — `oriente`
The traditional name for eastern Cuba, where tobacco is grown on a modest scale in the provinces around Santiago and Holguin. The leaf is generally strong and rustic and is used domestically rather than in export Habanos.

### Partido
` /par-TEE-do/ | Growing & Terroir` — `partido`
A small Cuban zone south of Havana, historically the island's premier shade-wrapper district, centered around San Antonio de los Banos. Urban expansion and disease have shrunk plantings drastically from their peak.

### Remedios
` /reh-MEH-dee-os/ | Growing & Terroir` — `remedios`
A central Cuban growing region, part of the broader Vuelta Arriba, spanning parts of Villa Clara and Sancti Spiritus. Its leaf is robust and full flavored but coarser than Vuelta Abajo tobacco, and it goes mostly into cigarettes and lower-tier cigars.

### San Andres Veracruz
` /san an-DRESS veh-ra-KROOS/ | Growing & Terroir` — `san-andres-veracruz`
The San Andres Tuxtla valley in Veracruz, Mexico, one of the oldest continuously farmed tobacco zones in the Americas. Its Negro San Andres leaf is thick and dark and is used chiefly as maduro wrapper and as flavoring filler.

### Semi Vuelta
` /SEH-mee VWEL-ta/ | Growing & Terroir` — `semi-vuelta`
A Cuban growing zone in central and eastern Pinar del Rio, adjacent to Vuelta Abajo but on heavier soils. Its plants produce larger, coarser, stronger leaf used mainly for filler and for the domestic cigarette and machine-made trade.

### Semillero
` /seh-mee-YEH-ro/ | Growing & Terroir` — `semillero`
The seedbed where tobacco is germinated before it goes to the field, traditionally a raised, sterilized bed under cloth and now often float trays in shallow water. Seedlings spend roughly six to eight weeks there.

### Suckering (Desahijado)
` /des-ah-ee-HAH-do/ | Growing & Terroir` — `suckering`
Removing the axillary shoots, called hijos or suckers, that a topped plant pushes out at every leaf joint. It is done by hand every few days, sometimes assisted by chemical growth inhibitors.

### Sun Grown
`Growing & Terroir` — `sun-grown`
Tobacco cultivated in open field without shade cloth. Full sun thickens the leaf, deepens color, raises oil and nicotine, and coarsens the veins.

### Talanga
` /ta-LAHN-ga/ | Growing & Terroir` — `talanga`
A valley north of Tegucigalpa in Honduras's Francisco Morazan department, at higher elevation and cooler than Jamastran. It is known for wrapper and for more refined, aromatic filler.

### Tapado
` /ta-PAH-do/ | Growing & Terroir` — `tapado`
Shade growing, from the Spanish for covered. Fields are tented with cheesecloth on posts and wire, which diffuses light, raises humidity, and holds heat, producing thinner, larger, more elastic leaves with finer veins.

### Terroir
` /tair-WAHR/ | Growing & Terroir` — `terroir`
The combined effect of soil, climate, altitude, water, and local growing practice on the character of a crop. In tobacco it explains why one seed variety produces different leaf in Jalapa than in Esteli or Pinar del Rio.

### Topping (Desbotonado)
` /des-bo-toh-NAH-do/ | Growing & Terroir` — `topping`
Cutting off the flower head once the plant is mature, so energy that would go into seed goes into the remaining leaves. The height at which a grower tops sets how many primings the plant will carry.

### Transplanting
`Growing & Terroir` — `transplanting`
Moving seedlings from the semillero into the field once they are roughly six to eight inches tall and have a workable root system. Plants are set in rows by hand, watered in, and spaced according to whether the crop is destined for wrapper or filler.

### Vega
` /VEH-ga/ | Growing & Terroir` — `vega`
A tobacco field or plantation, especially in Cuba, where the word carries the sense of a specific named plot with its own reputation. Vegas are identified by soil type, drainage, and the family that works them.

### Vega Fina
` /VEH-ga FEE-na/ | Growing & Terroir` — `vega-fina`
A top-rated tobacco plot, the Cuban designation for a vega whose leaf consistently qualifies for the highest grades. In Cuba only a limited number of plots in Vuelta Abajo hold this standing.

### Veguero
` /veh-GEH-ro/ | Growing & Terroir` — `veguero`
A tobacco farmer, specifically one who works a vega. In Cuba the veguero is a distinct social figure, often working land his family has farmed for generations under quota agreements with the state.

### Vuelta Abajo
` /VWEL-ta ah-BAH-ho/ | Growing & Terroir` — `vuelta-abajo`
The westernmost tobacco zone of Cuba's Pinar del Rio province, centered on San Juan y Martinez and San Luis. Its sandy, iron-rich soils and reliable dry season make it the only Cuban region that produces all leaf classes, wrapper included, at the top grade.

## Production & Curing — 36 terms

### Aging Room
`Production & Curing` — `aging-room`
The climate-controlled, usually cedar-lined room where finished cigars rest before packing, called an escaparate in Cuban factories. Conditions are typically held around the mid to upper 60s Fahrenheit with relative humidity in the mid to upper 60s.

### Air Curing
`Production & Curing` — `air-curing`
The standard cure for cigar tobacco: leaves are hung in an open barn and dried slowly by ambient air over roughly four to eight weeks, with no added heat. Chlorophyll breaks down, starches convert, and the leaf turns from green to yellow to brown.

### Bale Aging
`Production & Curing` — `bale-aging`
Holding fermented leaf in tercios for an extended period, from months to many years, in a warehouse kept at controlled humidity. Chemical change continues slowly, without the heat of active fermentation, and bales are periodically opened and inspected.

### Blending
`Production & Curing` — `blending`
The craft of combining leaves of different origins, primings, and ages so that the resulting cigar has the intended strength, aroma, burn, and development. Blenders work by rolling test cigars, smoking them, and adjusting one variable at a time.

### Box Aging
`Production & Curing` — `box-aging`
Aging cigars in their sealed original box, whether at the factory, a retailer, or a private humidor. The cigars sit in stable conditions and continue to exchange aroma with each other and with the cedar around them.

### Browning
`Production & Curing` — `browning`
The stage after yellowing, when oxidation of polyphenols turns the leaf tan to brown and the lamina dries. Stem drying follows, and the leaf is considered cured once the midrib is dry through.

### Casa de Tabaco
` /KAH-sa deh ta-BAH-ko/ | Production & Curing` — `casa-de-tabaco`
The curing barn, a tall wooden structure with a thatched or metal roof and adjustable doors and vents used to control airflow and humidity. Barns are sited and oriented so prevailing wind can be let in or shut out as the cure demands, and leaves hang in tiers on poles called cujes.

### Cuje
` /KOO-heh/ | Production & Curing` — `cuje`
A wooden pole used to hang strung or tied pairs of tobacco leaves inside a curing barn, letting air circulate around the leaf as it dries.

### Despalillo
` /des-pah-LEE-yo/ | Production & Curing` — `despalillo`
Destemming, the removal of the central vein from a leaf, done by hand for wrapper and binder and by hand or machine for filler. Full destemming splits the leaf into two halves; partial stripping removes only the thick lower portion of the vein.

### Escogida
` /es-ko-HEE-da/ | Production & Curing` — `escogida`
The sorting operation, and the building where it happens, in which cured and fermented leaves are separated by size, color, texture, and soundness. Workers, traditionally women called escogedoras, sort by eye and feel into dozens of piles.

### Factory Codes
`Production & Curing` — `factory-codes`
Stamps and marks on the bottom of a box identifying where and when it was made. Cuban boxes carry a factory code plus a month and year stamp, a system formalized in 2000, while non-Cuban makers use their own date and lot codes.

### Fermentation
`Production & Curing` — `fermentation`
The controlled bulk process in which cured leaf, moistened and stacked, heats from within through microbial activity and oxidation. Ammonia, nicotine, and harsh nitrogenous compounds are driven off while starches and proteins break down into gentler compounds.

### Fire Curing
`Production & Curing` — `fire-curing`
Curing over smoldering hardwood fires laid on the barn floor, which dries the leaf while depositing smoke phenols in it. The process runs from several days to several weeks depending on the intensity wanted.

### First Fermentation
`Production & Curing` — `first-fermentation`
The initial fermentation done shortly after curing, usually on the farm and often with the leaf still in hands and on the stem. It is comparatively short, from a few days to a few weeks, and is run at moderate temperature to drive off the worst of the ammonia.

### Flue Curing
`Production & Curing` — `flue-curing`
Curing in a sealed barn heated by pipes or heat exchangers, with temperature raised in stages over roughly a week so the leaf yellows then dries fast. It preserves natural sugars and is the standard method for bright Virginia cigarette tobacco.

### Half-Leaf
`Production & Curing` — `half-leaf`
One of the two lamina halves left after full destemming, and the standard unit in which wrapper and much binder is handled, sorted, and priced. A single wrapper is cut from a half-leaf, usually leaving a strip too small to use again.

### Leaf Breakage
`Production & Curing` — `leaf-breakage`
Tearing, cracking, or shattering of leaf during handling, most often because moisture is too low or the leaf was moved too roughly. Thick, brittle types such as Broadleaf and heavily fermented maduro wrapper break more readily than thin shade leaf.

### Ligada
` /lee-GAH-da/ | Production & Curing` — `ligada`
The blend recipe, the specific list of leaf grades, origins and quantities that make up a given cigar. A ligada names filler leaves by class and priming, plus the binder and wrapper, and is usually the most closely held document in a factory.

### Majagua
` /ma-HA-gwa/ | Production & Curing` — `majagua`
Fibrous inner bark, traditionally from the majagua tree, cut into strips and used to tie bundles of leaves, called hands or gavillas, and to secure bales. It is flexible, strong, and does not cut into the leaf.

### Marrying
`Production & Curing` — `marrying`
The rest period after rolling, during which the wrapper, binder, and several filler leaves in a finished cigar equalize in moisture and their aromas integrate. Factories typically hold cigars in cedar-lined rooms for weeks to months before boxing.

### Moja
` /MO-ha/ | Production & Curing` — `moja`
Re-wetting cured or fermented leaf, usually by spraying or dipping in water, to bring it back to a workable and fermentable moisture level. The Spanish verb mojar means to wet, and the noun names both the act and the water used.

### Pilon (Burro)
` /pee-LOHN / BOO-ro/ | Production & Curing` — `pilon`
The stack in which tobacco ferments, written pilon with an accent on the final syllable and also called a burro. Hands of leaf are laid in a rectangular or square pile several feet high, weighted by their own mass, so heat and moisture build in the core.

### Pilon Temperature Monitoring
` /pee-LOHN/ | Production & Curing` — `pilon-temperature-monitoring`
The practice of tracking the internal heat of a fermenting stack with long-stemmed thermometers pushed into the core. Delicate wrapper and thin leaf are generally held at the low end, roughly the mid 90s to around 110F (35 to 43C), while heavy filler and ligero are allowed to run considerably hotter, commonly into the 120s and 130s and sometimes approaching 140F (about 50 to 60C).

### Plume vs Mold
`Production & Curing` — `plume-vs-mold`
Plume, also called bloom, is a fine white crystalline residue of oils that migrate to the wrapper surface during long, stable aging; it brushes off cleanly and leaves no mark. Mold is a living growth, usually blue-green or gray, fuzzy, raised, patchy, and it stains the leaf and smells musty.

### Rezagado
` /reh-sa-GAH-do/ | Production & Curing` — `rezagado`
The fine grading of wrapper leaf specifically, in which leaves already sorted broadly are classified into precise categories by shade, size, thickness, and blemish. Cuban practice recognizes a long list of classes with traditional names.

### Second Fermentation
`Production & Curing` — `second-fermentation`
The main factory fermentation, carried out after destemming and initial sorting, in larger pilones and over a much longer span, commonly several weeks to several months with repeated turnings. Target temperatures are set by leaf class rather than by a single rule.

### Spanish Cedar Aging
`Production & Curing` — `spanish-cedar-aging`
Resting cigars in contact with Spanish cedar, botanically Cedrela odorata rather than a true cedar, used to line aging rooms, boxes, and humidors. The wood buffers humidity, repels tobacco beetles, and imparts a characteristic aroma.

### Stem (Vena)
` /VEH-na/ | Production & Curing` — `stem`
The central midrib of the tobacco leaf, along with the larger veins branching from it. Stem material is woody, low in flavor compounds, and burns unevenly, so it is removed from premium cigar leaf and often sold off for reconstituted sheet or fertilizer.

### Sun Curing
`Production & Curing` — `sun-curing`
Drying leaf on racks or lines in direct sunlight rather than in a barn. It is standard for oriental and some dark tobaccos and is used occasionally in the cigar world for filler and for candela-adjacent effects.

### Tercio
` /TAIR-see-oh/ | Production & Curing` — `tercio`
The traditional bale in which fermented tobacco is stored and shipped, made by packing hands of leaf into a square and wrapping them in sheets of royal palm bark. A tercio typically holds on the order of 50 to 80 kilograms.

### Third Fermentation
`Production & Curing` — `third-fermentation`
An additional fermentation given to specific grades after the main one, typically to heavy ligero or to leaf destined for maduro wrapper. The stack is re-wetted and rebuilt, often at lower temperature, for weeks or months more.

### Turning the Pilon
` /pee-LOHN/ | Production & Curing` — `turning-the-pilon`
Dismantling a fermenting stack and rebuilding it inside out, so leaf from the hot core moves to the cool outside and vice versa. Turning happens whenever the pile reaches its target temperature, which can be every few days early on and less often as activity slows.

### Wrapper Aging
`Production & Curing` — `wrapper-aging`
The separate, gentler aging given to wrapper leaf after its fermentation, usually in smaller bales or cases at controlled humidity. Wrapper is fermented cooler than filler and then rested, often for one to three years or more, to develop color and elasticity.

### Wrapper Yield
`Production & Curing` — `wrapper-yield`
The proportion of a crop that ends up usable as wrapper after curing, fermentation, sorting, and cutting. Even on a well-run shade farm only a minority of harvested leaf makes wrapper grade, with the rest falling to binder, filler, or waste.

### Yagua (Palm Bark)
` /YAH-gwa/ | Production & Curing` — `yagua`
The tough sheath from the trunk of the royal palm, used to wrap tercios. Yagua breathes, resists insects reasonably well, and holds a bale's shape under stacking.

### Yellowing
`Production & Curing` — `yellowing`
The first stage of curing, in which chlorophyll degrades and the leaf turns from green to yellow while still holding most of its moisture. It typically runs several days to about two weeks under warm, humid barn conditions.

## Storage & Aging — 19 terms

### Acclimation
` /ak-luh-MAY-shun/ | Storage & Aging` — `acclimation`
The settling period after cigars are moved between environments, during which their internal moisture equalizes with the new storage. Two weeks is a common minimum; a month or more is safer after shipping.

### Boveda Packs
` /boh-VAY-duh/ | Storage & Aging` — `boveda-packs`
Sealed two-way humidity control packets containing a saturated salt solution in a gel, sold at fixed RH targets such as 62, 65, 69, and 72 percent. They both release and absorb moisture to hold that setpoint.

### Cabinet Humidor
`Storage & Aging` — `cabinet-humidor`
A large freestanding or built-in humidor, often with drawers and shelves and sometimes with active electronic humidification and temperature control. Capacities run from several hundred to many thousands of cigars.

### Cigar Beetle
`Storage & Aging` — `cigar-beetle`
Lasioderma serricorne, the tobacco beetle, a small brown insect whose larvae bore through cigars and leave pinholes and dust. Dormant eggs are present in most tobacco and hatch reliably once temperatures rise above roughly 72 degrees Fahrenheit, or about 22 Celsius.

### Coolerdor
` /KOO-ler-dor/ | Storage & Aging` — `coolerdor`
A picnic cooler repurposed as a humidor. The insulated plastic shell already seals well and resists humidity swings, so only cedar trays and a humidification source need adding.

### Desktop Humidor
`Storage & Aging` — `desktop-humidor`
A tabletop wooden box, typically holding twenty-five to three hundred cigars, with a cedar lining and a passive humidification device. The most common form of home storage.

### Distilled Water
`Storage & Aging` — `distilled-water`
Water with dissolved minerals removed by distillation. It is the standard choice for charging humidification devices.

### Dry-Boxing
`Storage & Aging` — `dry-boxing`
Moving a cigar out of the humidor into a dry, unhumidified box or ordinary room air for a day or several days before smoking it.

### Freezing Protocol
`Storage & Aging` — `freezing-protocol`
A staged process for killing beetle eggs and larvae: seal cigars in an airtight bag, rest them in a refrigerator for about a day, move them to a freezer for roughly three days, return them to the refrigerator for a day, then let them come to room temperature before returning them to the humidor.

### Humidification Beads
`Storage & Aging` — `humidification-beads`
Silica or zeolite media engineered to hold a target relative humidity, charged with distilled water and reusable indefinitely. Common setpoints are 62, 65, and 70 percent.

### Humidor
` /HYOO-mih-dor/ | Storage & Aging` — `humidor`
A sealed container that holds cigars at a stable relative humidity, usually lined with Spanish cedar and fitted with some form of humidification. Sizes run from travel cases to walk-in rooms.

### Hygrometer
` /hy-GROM-uh-ter/ | Storage & Aging` — `hygrometer`
An instrument that measures relative humidity. Digital units are generally more accurate than the analog spring-and-needle type, but every hygrometer drifts and needs periodic checking.

### Over-Humidification
`Storage & Aging` — `over-humidification`
Storage at a relative humidity higher than a cigar burns well at, historically the 70 percent half of the old 70/70 rule. Symptoms include a spongy feel, tight draw, tunneling, and flat flavor.

### Relative Humidity
`Storage & Aging` — `relative-humidity`
The amount of water vapor in air expressed as a percentage of the maximum that air can hold at a given temperature. Abbreviated RH.

### Salt-Test Calibration
`Storage & Aging` — `salt-test-calibration`
A method of checking a hygrometer by sealing it with a slurry of table salt and distilled water, which produces a stable 75 percent RH environment at room temperature after several hours.

### Seasoning a Humidor
`Storage & Aging` — `seasoning-a-humidor`
Bringing a new humidor's cedar up to working moisture before storing cigars in it, typically by leaving a high-RH Boveda pack or a dish of distilled water inside for one to two weeks with no cigars present.

### Spanish Cedar
`Storage & Aging` — `spanish-cedar`
Cedrela odorata, a tropical hardwood unrelated to true cedar, used to line humidors and to make cigar boxes. It absorbs and releases moisture readily, resists insects, and contributes an aromatic note.

### Tupperdor
` /TUP-er-dor/ | Storage & Aging` — `tupperdor`
An airtight food storage container used for cigar storage, usually with a Boveda pack and a strip of cedar inside. Common for small collections, travel, and quarantine of new arrivals.

### Wineador
` /WY-nee-dor/ | Storage & Aging` — `wineador`
A thermoelectric wine refrigerator converted to cigar storage, usually refitted with Spanish cedar shelves or drawers.

## Tobacco & Leaf — 39 terms

### Arapiraca
` /ah-rah-pee-RAH-ka/ | Tobacco & Leaf` — `arapiraca`
Brazilian tobacco grown around Arapiraca in the state of Alagoas. Coarser and more robust than Mata Fina, it cures to a very dark brown and is used chiefly as a maduro-style wrapper.

### Barber Pole
`Tobacco & Leaf` — `barber-pole`
A cigar wrapped in two contrasting wrappers cut into strips and applied in an alternating spiral, most often a light natural against a dark maduro. Building one requires cutting and aligning both leaves precisely so the seams stay tight.

### Besuki
` /beh-SOO-kee/ | Tobacco & Leaf` — `besuki`
Indonesian leaf from the Besuki region of East Java, traditionally sold in the late-harvest Na-Oogst grade for binder and wrapper. Java-grown tobacco of this type is thin, mild, and quick burning, and it underpins much of the Dutch and German cigar trade.

### Cameroon
` /KAM-eh-roon/ | Tobacco & Leaf` — `cameroon`
Wrapper grown in Cameroon and the Central African Republic from Sumatra-derived seed, cultivated under natural cloud cover rather than cloth. The leaf is thin, notably toothy, and tan to brown with a distinctive nutty aroma.

### Centro Fino
` /SEN-tro FEE-no/ | Tobacco & Leaf` — `centro-fino`
The upper middle priming, above centro ligero and below centro gordo. Its leaves are fine grained and elastic, often the best material on a shade plant for wrapper.

### Centro Gordo
` /SEN-tro GOR-do/ | Tobacco & Leaf` — `centro-gordo`
Literally the thick center. This priming sits just below the corona and yields large, heavy, well-developed leaves that usually classify as ligero or heavy viso.

### Centro Ligero
` /SEN-tro lee-HEH-ro/ | Tobacco & Leaf` — `centro-ligero`
The lower middle priming, taken above uno y medio. Despite the name, the leaves are light rather than strong; ligero here means light in weight and color, not the filler grade.

### Claro
` /KLAH-ro/ | Tobacco & Leaf` — `claro`
A light tan or golden wrapper shade, typically from shade-grown leaf given gentle fermentation. The related trade term cristal is sometimes used for the palest, glossiest examples.

### Colorado
` /ko-lo-RAH-do/ | Tobacco & Leaf` — `colorado`
A medium reddish brown wrapper shade, sitting between Colorado Claro and maduro on the traditional color scale. Colorado Maduro is the next step darker.

### Colorado Claro
` /ko-lo-RAH-do KLAH-ro/ | Tobacco & Leaf` — `colorado-claro`
A light reddish brown wrapper shade, one step darker than claro and lighter than colorado. It is the classic tone of well-aged Cuban and Dominican wrappers.

### Connecticut Broadleaf
`Tobacco & Leaf` — `connecticut-broadleaf`
A thick, sun-grown Connecticut variety harvested by cutting the whole stalk rather than by priming. Its heavy, rugged leaf takes long fermentation well and becomes a dark, sweet maduro wrapper.

### Connecticut Shade
`Tobacco & Leaf` — `connecticut-shade`
Wrapper grown under cheesecloth tents in the Connecticut River Valley, developed in the early twentieth century from Cuban and Sumatran stock. Shading yields a thin, silky, pale golden leaf with fine veins.

### Corojo
` /ko-ROH-ho/ | Tobacco & Leaf` — `corojo`
A Cuban variety developed in the mid twentieth century at the El Corojo vega in Pinar del Rio and grown under shade as Cuba's standard wrapper for decades. Extreme susceptibility to blue mold and black shank pushed Cuba to retire it in favor of hybrids in the 1990s.

### Corojo 99
` /ko-ROH-ho noh-VEN-ta ee NWEH-veh/ | Tobacco & Leaf` — `corojo-99`
A disease-resistant Cuban hybrid released around 1999 to replace classic Corojo as a shade-grown wrapper variety. It keeps much of the parent's leaf structure while tolerating blue mold far better.

### Corona (Priming)
` /ko-ROH-na/ | Tobacco & Leaf` — `corona`
The topmost regular priming on a sun-grown tobacco plant, harvested last. Corona leaves are small, thick, and strong, and they grade out as ligero in most sorting rooms. The same word also names a cigar size, which is a separate usage.

### Criollo
` /kree-OH-yo/ | Tobacco & Leaf` — `criollo`
Spanish for native or creole, and the traditional name for the old Cuban filler variety grown in the sun. Historic criollo was used for filler and binder while corojo supplied wrapper; the original strain has largely been replaced by improved selections.

### Criollo 98
` /kree-OH-yo noh-VEN-ta ee OH-cho/ | Tobacco & Leaf` — `criollo-98`
A Cuban selection released in the late 1990s by the island's tobacco research institute to restore criollo character with better disease resistance, particularly to blue mold. It is grown in the sun for filler and binder and has been widely adopted outside Cuba.

### Cristal
` /krees-TAHL/ | Tobacco & Leaf` — `cristal`
A trade term for an exceptionally pale, glossy, thin wrapper, essentially the lightest end of the claro range. Usage varies between factories and it has never been a standardized grade.

### Double Claro (Candela)
` /KAHN-deh-la/ | Tobacco & Leaf` — `double-claro`
A green wrapper, also called candela or AMS for American Market Selection, produced by harvesting early and curing fast with heat so chlorophyll is locked in rather than broken down. The barn cycle takes days rather than weeks.

### Ecuadorian Habano
`Tobacco & Leaf` — `ecuadorian-habano`
Cuban-seed wrapper grown in Ecuador's coastal lowlands, where near-constant cloud cover acts as natural shade. The result is a leaf with Habano spice but thinner, more elastic, and more evenly colored than sun-grown Habano elsewhere.

### Ecuadorian Sumatra
`Tobacco & Leaf` — `ecuadorian-sumatra`
Sumatra-seed wrapper grown in Ecuador under the same natural cloud cover that shades the country's Habano crop. It is thin and fine grained, darker and sweeter than Connecticut Shade but milder than Habano.

### Habano 2000
` /ah-BAH-no dos MEEL/ | Tobacco & Leaf` — `habano-2000`
A Cuban hybrid, also written Habana 2000, released in the late 1990s for resistance to blue mold and black shank. It is grown both under shade for wrapper and in the sun for filler, and yields more usable leaf per plant than the varieties it replaced.

### Habano Seed
` /ah-BAH-no/ | Tobacco & Leaf` — `habano-seed`
A broad label for Cuban-descended tobacco varieties grown outside Cuba, covering everything from Corojo and Criollo lines to their modern hybrids. It describes ancestry rather than a specific cultivar.

### Kentucky Fire-Cured
`Tobacco & Leaf` — `kentucky-fire-cured`
Dark tobacco cured over smoldering hardwood fires in closed barns, a process that drives smoke phenols deep into the leaf. Traditionally grown for snuff and chewing tobacco, it entered premium cigars in the 2010s.

### Libre de Pie
` /LEE-breh deh pee-EH/ | Tobacco & Leaf` — `libre-de-pie`
The bottom priming, literally free of the foot, meaning the leaves nearest the ground. Harvested first, they are thin, sandy, often damaged, and grade as volado.

### Maduro
` /ma-DOO-ro/ | Tobacco & Leaf` — `maduro`
A wrapper color class, dark brown to nearly black, produced by using thick leaf and giving it longer or hotter fermentation. Maduro is a process and color designation, not a tobacco variety, and it is often labeled SMS in the old market-selection shorthand.

### Mata Fina
` /MAH-ta FEE-na/ | Tobacco & Leaf` — `mata-fina`
Brazilian tobacco from the Reconcavo region of Bahia, named for its growing area. It is dark, oily, and naturally sweet, fermented long and used mostly as wrapper and as a flavoring filler.

### Natural (EMS)
`Tobacco & Leaf` — `natural`
The broad middle of the wrapper color range, from light tan through medium brown, marked EMS for English Market Selection in the old three-part shorthand of AMS, EMS, and SMS. Natural simply means the leaf was cured and fermented conventionally, without candela treatment or extended maduro fermentation.

### Nicotiana Tabacum
` /nik-oh-she-AH-na ta-BAH-kum/ | Tobacco & Leaf` — `nicotiana-tabacum`
The cultivated tobacco species from which all cigar tobacco comes. A member of the nightshade family, it arose as a natural hybrid of two South American Nicotiana species and exists today as hundreds of named varieties and cultivars.

### Olor Dominicano
` /oh-LOR do-mee-nee-KAH-no/ | Tobacco & Leaf` — `olor-dominicano`
A Dominican variety long established on the island, with large, thin, quick-burning leaves. Olor is milder than Piloto Cubano and is used mainly as binder and as a combustion-friendly filler component.

### Oscuro
` /os-KOO-ro/ | Tobacco & Leaf` — `oscuro`
The darkest wrapper class, deeper than maduro and often close to black, achieved with the thickest top-priming leaf and the longest fermentation. Some oscuro wrappers are also left on the plant longer before harvest.

### Pelo de Oro
` /PEH-lo deh OH-ro/ | Tobacco & Leaf` — `pelo-de-oro`
An old Cuban variety whose name means hair of gold, celebrated for intense, distinctive flavor and notorious for its vulnerability to blue mold and other disease. Cuba effectively stopped commercial cultivation of it, though growers in Nicaragua and elsewhere have revived small plantings.

### Pennsylvania Broadleaf
`Tobacco & Leaf` — `pennsylvania-broadleaf`
Sun-grown seedleaf tobacco from Lancaster County and surrounding Pennsylvania farmland, historically grown for chewing and cigar filler. The leaf is dark, thick, and forceful, with earthy sweetness and high nicotine.

### Piloto Cubano
` /pee-LOH-toh koo-BAH-no/ | Tobacco & Leaf` — `piloto-cubano`
The dominant Dominican filler variety, grown from Cuban seed carried to the Cibao Valley around the 1960s and adapted there for decades. It produces a rich, slightly sweet filler with good body.

### Rosado
` /ro-SAH-do/ | Tobacco & Leaf` — `rosado`
A wrapper with a distinctly reddish or rosy cast, from the Spanish for pinkish. It is a description of hue rather than a variety or a formal grade, and it usually comes from Cuban-seed leaf grown in strong sun.

### San Vicente
` /san vee-SEN-teh/ | Tobacco & Leaf` — `san-vicente`
A Dominican selection derived from Piloto Cubano, developed to improve yield and disease resistance while keeping Piloto's character. It is grown widely in the Cibao Valley for filler and binder.

### Sumatra
` /soo-MAH-tra/ | Tobacco & Leaf` — `sumatra`
A tobacco type originating on the Indonesian island of Sumatra and developed under Dutch colonial trade for thin, elastic, fine-grained wrappers. Sumatra seed is now grown in Ecuador, Nicaragua, Honduras, and Brazil as well as Indonesia.

### Uno y Medio
` /OO-no ee MEH-dee-oh/ | Tobacco & Leaf` — `uno-y-medio`
The second priming from the bottom, named one and a half. These leaves are thin and pale, generally sorted into volado or light seco.

### Viso
` /VEE-so/ | Tobacco & Leaf` — `viso`
In Nicaraguan and Dominican practice, the filler priming that sits between seco and ligero: fuller than seco, more combustible than ligero, often glossy with oil. Cuban wrapper sorters historically used viso for oily, sheened leaves rather than for a priming.

## Construction — 27 terms  (index only — fetch on demand)

### Accordion Bunch
`Construction` — `accordion-bunch`
Each filler leaf is folded back and forth into pleats, and the pleated leaves are then gathered into the bunch. The folds leave vertical air passages along the cigar's length.

### Book/Libro Bunching
` /LEE-broh/ | Construction` — `book-libro-bunching`
Filler leaves are laid flat on top of one another and the stack is either rolled up like a scroll or folded shut like a book. Speed has made it the common method in Dominican and Nicaraguan factories.

### Bunch Density
`Construction` — `bunch-density`
How tightly filler sits inside the binder, judged by feel and confirmed by draw measurement. Some styles vary density deliberately along the cigar, leaving the foot slightly looser.

### Buncher vs Roller
`Construction` — `buncher-vs-roller`
The division of labor between the person who forms and molds the bunch and the person who applies the wrapper. A traditional Cuban torcedor does both; most Central American factories separate the roles.

### Bunching
`Construction` — `bunching`
The stage in which filler leaves are gathered, arranged, and enclosed in the binder to form the cigar's core. Draw, burn, and density are all settled here, before any wrapper is applied.

### Casquillo
` /kahs-KEE-yoh/ | Construction` — `casquillo`
The small round or oval piece of wrapper cut to finish the cap, punched from wrapper offcuts with the chaveta and fixed over the head with a touch of gum.

### Chaveta
` /chah-VEH-tah/ | Construction` — `chaveta`
The roller's crescent-shaped, handleless blade, used to cut wrapper and binder to shape and to trim the foot. It is held flat against the board and worked with a rocking motion.

### Chupi
` /CHOO-pee/ | Construction` — `chupi`
A small, circular hand blade used to cut a perfectly round piece of wrapper leaf for capping the head of a cigar.

### Draw Machine
`Construction` — `draw-machine`
A bench instrument that pulls a fixed airflow through a cigar and reports the pressure drop, usually in millimeters or inches of water column. Cuban factories adopted them in the early 2000s alongside manual checks.

### Draw Test
`Construction` — `draw-test`
A check of airflow resistance through a bunch or a finished cigar, made by mouth by the roller or supervisor, or mechanically on a gauge. Cigars outside the accepted range are reworked or rejected.

### Empune
` /em-POO-neh/ | Construction` — `empune`
A bunch of filler leaves already wrapped in a binder leaf, the intermediate stage between bunching and setting the cigar into a mold for pressing.

### Entubado
` /en-too-BAH-doh/ | Construction` — `entubado`
A bunching method in which every filler leaf is rolled into its own small tube before the tubes are gathered together. The finished bunch contains a set of parallel channels running the length of the cigar.

### Escaparate
` /es-kah-pah-RAH-teh/ | Construction` — `escaparate`
The factory's cedar-lined conditioning room, where finished cigars rest for weeks while moisture equalizes and the gum sets. Temperature and humidity are held steady and cigars are stacked in wheels or half-wheels.

### Flag/Pigtail Cap Application
`Construction` — `flag-pigtail-cap-application`
A finish in which the wrapper is left long at the head and either laid flat across the cap as a flag or twisted into a short pigtail. Traditional on Cuban lanceros and widely adopted elsewhere as a house signature.

### Goma Tragacanto (Vegetable Gum)
` /GOH-mah trah-gah-KAHN-toh/ | Construction` — `goma-tragacanto`
The odorless, flavorless plant gum used to fix the wrapper's final seam and the cap. Traditionally gum tragacanth from Astragalus shrubs, though many factories now run a comparable cellulose-based adhesive.

### Hot Spot
`Construction` — `hot-spot`
A point that burns faster than the tobacco around it, producing a run or a canoe up one side. It usually reflects uneven filler density or a thin patch in binder or wrapper.

### Lieberman Bunching
`Construction` — `lieberman-bunching`
A semi-mechanized bunching method in which a machine gathers and shapes the filler leaves before a human roller applies the binder and wrapper by hand.

### Machine-Made
`Construction` — `machine-made`
A cigar built by machine rather than by hand, typically from chopped or shredded short-filler tobacco measured by weight onto a moving binder, sometimes wrapped in homogenized tobacco leaf rather than a single whole leaf.

### Mold (Molde)
` /MOHL-deh/ | Construction` — `mold`
A two-piece wooden or plastic form with cavities cut to a specific vitola, into which bunches are laid to set their shape. Bunches sit under press for roughly forty-five minutes to an hour and are rotated partway through.

### Plancha
` /PLAHN-chah/ | Construction` — `plancha`
An individual roller's workstation - the bench position with its board, blade, gum pot, and allotted molds. The word also covers the day's output from that station.

### Plug
`Construction` — `plug`
A localized blockage in the filler that stops or badly restricts draw. Common causes are a folded leaf, a piece of stem left in the tripa, or over-compression during bunching or pressing.

### Press
`Construction` — `press`
The screw or hydraulic frame that applies pressure to stacked molds. Pressure and duration decide whether a cigar emerges round, softly squared, or sharply box-pressed.

### Rolling
`Construction` — `rolling`
Application of the wrapper leaf to a bunched and molded cigar, spiraled from foot to head and finished with a cap. Where labor is split, this is the second half of the job, performed after the buncher.

### Tabla
` /TAH-blah/ | Construction` — `tabla`
The flat wooden rolling board on a torcedor's bench, where leaf is cut and cigars are formed. Hardwood is standard, worn smooth over years of use.

### Torcedor Pairs (Parejas)
` /tor-seh-DOR pah-REH-hahs/ | Construction` — `torcedor-pairs`
A two-person team in which one worker bunches and the other wraps, splitting construction across two benches. The arrangement is standard in high-volume factories outside Cuba.

### Triple Cap Application
`Construction` — `triple-cap-application`
A Cuban finishing technique laying three separate pieces of wrapper over the head, the last of them the casquillo, which leaves three visible seam lines. Non-Cuban factories more often apply a single cap disc.

### Wrapper Pairing
`Construction` — `wrapper-pairing`
Matching wrappers by color and sheen across the cigars destined for one box so the top layer reads uniform. Sorters work under consistent light and may distinguish dozens of shades.

## Culture & Collecting — 22 terms  (index only — fetch on demand)

### 8-9-8 Box
`Culture & Collecting` — `8-9-8-box`
A packing format of twenty-five cigars arranged in three rows of eight, nine, and eight, usually in a curved or bowed box. The arrangement lets round cigars nest without pressing each other flat.

### Aficionado
` /ah-fee-see-oh-NAH-doh/ | Culture & Collecting` — `aficionado`
A devoted enthusiast. In cigars the word denotes serious interest and accumulated knowledge rather than any formal standing, and it is also the title of the field's best known magazine.

### Ash-Tapping Etiquette
`Culture & Collecting` — `ash-tapping-etiquette`
The convention of letting ash build and then rolling or gently tapping it off into an ashtray rather than striking the cigar sharply. Common practice is to let roughly an inch accumulate before removing it.

### Authentication
`Culture & Collecting` — `authentication`
The process of determining whether a box of cigars is genuine, using construction details, band and seal printing, box codes, holograms, barcode features, and the smoking characteristics of the cigars themselves.

### Box Split
`Culture & Collecting` — `box-split`
An arrangement in which several people jointly buy a box and divide the cigars, sharing cost and reducing the risk of committing to a full box of an unfamiliar blend.

### Cabinet Selection (SLB)
`Culture & Collecting` — `cabinet-selection`
A plain, unvarnished cedar box, usually with a sliding lid, in which cigars are packed loosely in bundles tied with silk ribbon rather than seated in trays. SLB stands for slide lid box.

### Cigar Dinner
`Culture & Collecting` — `cigar-dinner`
A ticketed event pairing courses of food and drink with cigars, usually hosted by a retailer or restaurant with a manufacturer's representative present. Attendees typically receive cigars as part of the price.

### Cigar Label Art
`Culture & Collecting` — `cigar-label-art`
The lithographed imagery used on cigar boxes and bands, at its height from the 1860s through the 1920s, using multi-stone chromolithography with gold embossing. Known in the trade by terms such as inner lid label, or vista.

### Cigar Lounge
`Culture & Collecting` — `cigar-lounge`
A room or business where smoking cigars is permitted, ranging from a retailer's back room with a few chairs to a licensed private club with lockers and a bar.

### Cohoba
` /koh-OH-bah/ | Culture & Collecting` — `cohoba`
The Taino ceremonial rite in which a cacique or religious specialist inhaled powdered tobacco through a Y-shaped pipe, inducing a trance state believed to allow communication with ancestral spirits.

### Counterfeit Cubans
`Culture & Collecting` — `counterfeit-cubans`
Fake Cuban cigars, made either from inferior tobacco or from non-Cuban leaf and sold in copied packaging. They are heavily concentrated in tourist markets, including street sales in Havana itself.

### Dress Box
`Culture & Collecting` — `dress-box`
A finished, decorated cigar box, typically covered in printed paper with a hinged lid and interior trim. The standard retail packaging for most premium cigars.

### Estanco
` /ehs-TAHN-koh/ | Culture & Collecting` — `estanco`
A small, licensed tobacco shop, historically operating under a Spanish crown monopoly and still the ordinary Spanish word for a tobacconist today.

### Festival del Habano
` /fes-tee-VAL del ah-BAH-noh/ | Culture & Collecting` — `festival-del-habano`
The annual Habanos trade and enthusiast event held in Havana, most years since 1999 and usually in late February. It includes factory and plantation visits, a trade fair, new product launches, a rollers' competition, and a closing gala auction of decorated humidors.

### Herf
` /hurf/ | Culture & Collecting` — `herf`
An informal gathering of cigar smokers. The term arose in the online cigar community of the 1990s and covers everything from four people on a porch to a several-hundred-person event.

### La Casa del Habano
` /lah KAH-sah del ah-BAH-noh/ | Culture & Collecting` — `la-casa-del-habano`
The official franchised retail network for Cuban cigars, established in the 1990s and operating in dozens of countries outside the United States. Shops meet Habanos standards for storage, staff training, and product range.

### Lounge Etiquette
`Culture & Collecting` — `lounge-etiquette`
The informal rules of conduct in cigar lounges: do not smoke another person's cigar or handle it, ask before joining a conversation, keep phone calls outside, do not wear strong fragrance, and buy from the shop whose chairs you are using.

### Pre-Embargo
`Culture & Collecting` — `pre-embargo`
Cuban cigars imported into the United States before the 1962 embargo took effect. Genuine examples are rare, and after six decades their condition varies enormously.

### Provenance
` /PROV-uh-nunce/ | Culture & Collecting` — `provenance`
The documented history of a specific box or cigar: where it was bought, when, and how it has been stored since. Established through receipts, box codes, photographs, and seller reputation.

### Semi Boite Nature
` /seh-mee bwaht nah-TOOR/ | Culture & Collecting` — `semi-boite-nature`
A partially finished cedar box, usually with a nailed rather than sliding lid and minimal decoration, sitting between a plain cabinet and a full dress box. Abbreviated SBN.

### Vintage Cigars
`Culture & Collecting` — `vintage-cigars`
Cigars aged well beyond release, typically a decade or more, kept in original boxes. Aging tends to soften strength and pepper and to bring forward leather, cedar, and sweetness, though not every blend improves and some fade.

### Vitolphily
` /vy-TOL-fih-lee/ | Culture & Collecting` — `vitolphily`
The collecting and study of cigar bands, and more broadly of cigar box labels and related ephemera. A collector is a vitolphilist.

## Industry & Trade — 25 terms  (index only — fetch on demand)

### Anejado
` /ah-nyeh-HAH-doh/ | Industry & Trade` — `anejado`
A Habanos program introduced in 2015 in which finished cigars, rather than the leaf, are aged in their boxes for a period of years before release. Spanish for aged.

### Boutique Brand
` /boo-TEEK/ | Industry & Trade` — `boutique-brand`
A small-production cigar company, usually without its own factory, that contracts production to an established fabrica. Annual output may be a few hundred thousand cigars or fewer.

### Box Code
`Industry & Trade` — `box-code`
The stamped or printed code on the underside of a cigar box identifying its origin and production date. On Cuban boxes it combines a factory code with a month and year code.

### Cubatabaco
` /koo-bah-tah-BAH-koh/ | Industry & Trade` — `cubatabaco`
The Cuban state tobacco enterprise, founded in 1962 after the nationalization of the industry. It handled all Cuban tobacco export until Habanos S.A. assumed the international marketing role in the 1990s; Cubatabaco retains domestic and trademark functions.

### Date Code
`Industry & Trade` — `date-code`
The portion of a box code giving production month and year. Cuba adopted a printed three-letter month code with a two-digit year in 2000; earlier boxes used other systems, and non-Cuban makers use their own formats.

### Denominacion de Origen Protegida
` /deh-noh-mee-nah-see-OWN deh oh-REE-hen proh-teh-HEE-dah/ | Industry & Trade` — `denominacion-de-origen-protegida`
Protected Denomination of Origin, abbreviated D.O.P. Applied to Cuban tobacco, it restricts the designation Habano to cigars made entirely in Cuba from Cuban tobacco under defined standards, and further protects specific growing zones such as Vuelta Abajo.

### Edicion Limitada
` /eh-dee-see-OWN lee-mee-TAH-dah/ | Industry & Trade` — `edicion-limitada`
A Habanos program, running since 2000, releasing a small number of Cuban cigars each year in sizes not otherwise in the regular range, using wrapper leaf aged at least two years. Bands are black and gold.

### Edicion Regional
` /eh-dee-see-OWN reh-hee-oh-NAL/ | Industry & Trade` — `edicion-regional`
A Habanos program, begun in 2005, in which a national or regional distributor commissions a limited run of a specific vitola for its own market, drawn from second-tier marcas rather than the global brands.

### Fabrica
` /FAH-bree-kah/ | Industry & Trade` — `fabrica`
Spanish for factory. In cigar usage it means the facility where cigars are made, as distinct from the farm and the curing barn.

### Factory Code
`Industry & Trade` — `factory-code`
A code identifying the factory that produced a box of cigars. Cuban factory codes are letter combinations that have been reassigned over time and were long treated as a trade secret, with published keys circulating among collectors.

### Factory Seconds
`Industry & Trade` — `factory-seconds`
Cigars rejected from a brand's regular production for cosmetic flaws or construction inconsistency, then sold unbanded or under a generic label at a discount. Also called seconds or overruns.

### FDA Deeming Rule
`Industry & Trade` — `fda-deeming-rule`
The 2016 United States regulation, effective in August of that year, that extended the Food and Drug Administration's tobacco authority to cigars, pipe tobacco, and vapor products. In 2023 a federal district court vacated the rule as applied to premium cigars, a decision upheld on appeal, and subsequent orders have addressed how premium cigars are defined for that exemption.

### Galera
` /gah-LEH-rah/ | Industry & Trade` — `galera`
The rolling gallery of a cigar factory, the large open room where torcedores work at rows of benches.

### Gran Reserva
` /grahn reh-SEHR-vah/ | Industry & Trade` — `gran-reserva`
The highest Habanos aging designation, using leaf aged roughly five years before rolling. Releases are infrequent, numbered, and small, typically five thousand boxes or fewer.

### Habanos S.A.
` /ah-BAH-nohs/ | Industry & Trade` — `habanos-s-a`
The company that holds worldwide marketing and distribution rights to Cuban cigars. Formed in the 1990s, it has long operated as a joint venture between the Cuban state and a foreign partner, with ownership on the non-Cuban side changing hands over the years.

### IPCPR
`Industry & Trade` — `ipcpr`
The International Premium Cigar and Pipe Retailers Association, the name the RTDA took in the mid-2000s and used until the 2019 rebranding to the Premium Cigar Association.

### Lector
` /lek-TOR/ | Industry & Trade` — `lector`
The reader who sits above the rolling floor and reads aloud to the workers, traditionally newspapers in the morning and novels in the afternoon. The practice began in Cuban factories in the 1860s and continues in some of them.

### MSRP
`Industry & Trade` — `msrp`
Manufacturer's Suggested Retail Price. In the United States the actual shelf price also reflects state and local tobacco excise taxes, which vary widely, so the same cigar can differ substantially in price between states.

### Premium Cigar Association
`Industry & Trade` — `premium-cigar-association`
The principal United States trade association for premium cigar and pipe retailers and manufacturers, abbreviated PCA. It adopted the name in 2019, succeeding the IPCPR, and runs the industry's largest annual trade show.

### Reserva
` /reh-SEHR-vah/ | Industry & Trade` — `reserva`
A Habanos designation for cigars made from leaf aged approximately three years before rolling, released in limited numbered runs with distinctive black and silver banding.

### RTDA
`Industry & Trade` — `rtda`
The Retail Tobacco Dealers of America, founded in 1933, the original name of the trade body that became the IPCPR and later the Premium Cigar Association.

### Substantial Equivalence
`Industry & Trade` — `substantial-equivalence`
The FDA pathway by which a tobacco product introduced after the statutory predicate date of February 15, 2007 may be marketed if it is shown to be substantially equivalent to a product on the market on that date.

### Tabaquero
` /tah-bah-KEH-roh/ | Industry & Trade` — `tabaquero`
A general Spanish term for a tobacco worker. Depending on context it can mean a roller, a factory worker, or anyone whose trade is tobacco.

### The Cuban Embargo
`Industry & Trade` — `the-cuban-embargo`
The United States trade embargo against Cuba, imposed in stages beginning in 1960 and 1962 and codified by later legislation. It prohibits the commercial importation of Cuban cigars into the United States; a personal-import allowance opened during the 2014 to 2016 thaw was rescinded in 2020.

### Torcedor
` /tor-say-DOR/ | Industry & Trade` — `torcedor`
A cigar roller. The word is Spanish for one who twists, and it refers specifically to the worker who bunches filler, applies the binder, and rolls the wrapper.

## Smoking & Technique — 24 terms  (index only — fetch on demand)

### Ash Retention
`Smoking & Technique` — `ash-retention`
How long a cigar's ash holds together before falling. Retention reflects leaf structure and mineral content of the soil more than blend quality.

### Burn Line
`Smoking & Technique` — `burn-line`
The boundary between ash and unburned tobacco, visible as a ring around the cigar. Its shape reports on construction, humidity, and smoking pace.

### Butane
` /BYOO-tayn/ | Smoking & Technique` — `butane`
The fuel used in cigar lighters. Refined, multiply filtered butane is preferred because impurities in cheaper fuel can leave a detectable taste and clog the burner.

### Canoeing
` /kuh-NOO-ing/ | Smoking & Technique` — `canoeing`
An uneven burn in which one side runs ahead of the other, hollowing the cigar into a shape resembling a canoe. Causes include wind, uneven lighting, and moisture variation across the leaves.

### Cedar Spill
`Smoking & Technique` — `cedar-spill`
A thin strip of Spanish cedar, often torn from the sheet inside a box, lit and used to light the cigar. A traditional alternative to lighters and matches.

### Cigar Shears
`Smoking & Technique` — `cigar-shears`
Scissors made specifically for cigars, with short curved blades that meet in a shallow arc. Quality shears are ground to close cleanly along the full edge.

### Cut
`Smoking & Technique` — `cut`
The removal of part of the cap so air can pass through the head of the cigar. Depth matters more than method: the goal is to open the cap without cutting past the shoulder, where the wrapper is anchored.

### Cutting a Figurado
` /fee-goo-RAH-doh/ | Smoking & Technique` — `cutting-a-figurado`
Opening a tapered head, as on a torpedo, belicoso, or perfecto. Because the cigar narrows toward the tip, cut depth changes the effective ring gauge of the opening.

### Draw
`Smoking & Technique` — `draw`
The resistance a cigar offers when you pull air through it. Draw is set by how tightly the bunch was rolled, how the filler leaves were folded, and whether the cap was cut cleanly.

### Even Burn
`Smoking & Technique` — `even-burn`
A burn line that advances squarely and perpendicular to the length of the cigar. Considered a marker of sound construction and appropriate humidity.

### Guillotine Cut
`Smoking & Technique` — `guillotine-cut`
A straight cut made with a single or double blade that removes a flat disc from the cap. Double-blade cutters close from both sides and tend to crush less than single-blade models.

### Long Wooden Matches
`Smoking & Technique` — `long-wooden-matches`
Extended matches, typically four inches or longer, sold for fireplaces and cigars. The length allows the sulphur head to burn off completely before the flame reaches the tobacco.

### Open Draw
`Smoking & Technique` — `open-draw`
A draw with very little resistance, common in underfilled or loosely bunched cigars. Air moves through freely and smoke volume is high.

### Punch Cut
`Smoking & Technique` — `punch-cut`
A circular blade that bores a hole in the center of the cap, leaving the rest of the cap intact. Punches are sized roughly 7 to 12 millimeters.

### Purging
`Smoking & Technique` — `purging`
Blowing gently backward through the cigar to expel stale smoke and tar from the head and the coal, typically done when the cigar has sat idle or tastes harsh.

### Relight
`Smoking & Technique` — `relight`
Relighting a cigar that has gone out. Best practice is to knock off the dead ash, purge the stale smoke, and toast the exposed foot before drawing.

### Smoking Cadence
`Smoking & Technique` — `smoking-cadence`
The rhythm of puffing, usually described as one draw every thirty to sixty seconds. Faster puffing raises coal temperature; slower puffing risks the cigar going out.

### Soft Flame
`Smoking & Technique` — `soft-flame`
A conventional yellow, low-pressure flame, as produced by a standard butane lighter, a match, or a spill. It burns cooler and less concentrated than a jet.

### Thirds
`Smoking & Technique` — `thirds`
The convention of describing a cigar in three roughly equal stages: first third, second third, and final third. Reviewers use it to track how a blend develops.

### Tight Draw
`Smoking & Technique` — `tight-draw`
A draw that requires excessive effort, caused by overfilled bunches, a bunched or folded filler leaf, or too small a cut. Severe cases are called plugged.

### Toasting the Foot
`Smoking & Technique` — `toasting-the-foot`
Warming the exposed foot of the cigar with a flame held just off the tobacco, rotating until the rim glows evenly, before drawing any smoke.

### Torch Flame
`Smoking & Technique` — `torch-flame`
A pressurized jet flame, blue and roaring, produced by forcing butane through a nozzle. Single, double, and triple jet lighters are common.

### Tunneling
`Smoking & Technique` — `tunneling`
A burn defect in which the filler burns faster than the wrapper and binder, so the coal recedes into a tube while the outer leaves lag behind.

### V-Cut
`Smoking & Technique` — `v-cut`
A wedge or notch cut that removes a V-shaped channel from the cap. Also called a cat's eye.

A figurado whose head tapers to a short rounded point rather than a sharp one, traditionally on a corona-length body. Most modern examples fall under the Cuban galera size Campanas, 140 mm by 52.

Cuban galera name for the belicoso figurado, 140 mm by 52; the word means bell. Bolivar Belicosos Finos and Romeo y Julieta Belicosos are both made in it.

` /kah-sah-DOH-res/ | Vitolas & Shapes` — `cazadores`
Cuban galera size of 162 mm by 43, historically a working man's cigar sold unbanded and in bundles. Only a few marcas still list it.

### Culebra
` /koo-LEH-brah/ | Vitolas & Shapes` — `culebra`

### Diadema
` /dee-ah-DEH-mah/ | Vitolas & Shapes` — `diadema`
Largest of the classic figurados, generally 8 inches or more, with a tapered head and frequently a closed or partly closed foot. Historically it served as much for display as for smoking.

### Double Corona
` /koh-ROH-nah/ | Vitolas & Shapes` — `double-corona`

### Double Robusto
` /roh-BOOS-toh/ | Vitolas & Shapes` — `double-robusto`
A commercial name for a robusto lengthened to roughly 6 or 6 1/2 inches while keeping 50 to 54 ring gauge. It is not a Cuban galera size and in practice overlaps most toros.

### Entreacto
` /en-treh-AHK-toh/ | Vitolas & Shapes` — `entreacto`
A very small Cuban format of 100 mm by 30, its name meaning intermission. Partagas is the marca most closely associated with it.

### Figurado
` /fee-goo-RAH-doh/ | Vitolas & Shapes` — `figurado`
Umbrella term for any cigar whose body departs from a straight cylinder, taking in perfectos, piramides, torpedoes, culebras, and diademas. Shaped molds and freehand skill are both required, so figurados fall to the senior torcedores.

### Gordo
` /GOR-doh/ | Vitolas & Shapes` — `gordo`
A thick parejo of 60 ring gauge or more, most often 6 to 6 1/2 inches long. Shops also call it a sixty, and Cuban production uses the format only in limited editions.

### Gran Corona
` /koh-ROH-nah/ | Vitolas & Shapes` — `gran-corona`
In the Cuban vitolario, an exceptionally long parejo at 235 mm by 47, made famous by the Montecristo A. Outside Cuba the term gets applied loosely to any oversized corona.

### Half Corona
`Vitolas & Shapes` — `half-corona`
A short parejo of about 3 1/2 inches by 44, formalized as a Cuban galera size at 90 mm by 44. H. Upmann's version put the name into general use.

` /er-MOH-soh/ | Vitolas & Shapes` — `hermoso-no-4`
Cuban galera size of 127 mm by 48, sold as Epicure No. 2 by Hoyo de Monterrey and under other names elsewhere. In effect a robusto with two fewer ring gauge and a slightly longer body.

### Julieta No. 2
` /hoo-lee-EH-tah/ | Vitolas & Shapes` — `julieta-no-2`
The Cuban galera name for the Churchill, 178 mm by 47. It borrows its name from Romeo y Julieta, whose Churchill established the size.

` /lah-GEE-toh/ | Vitolas & Shapes` — `laguito-no-1`
The Cuban galera name for the lancero size, 192 mm by 38, developed at the El Laguito factory for Cohiba in the 1960s. It is traditionally finished with a pigtail cap.

### Laguito No. 2
` /lah-GEE-toh/ | Vitolas & Shapes` — `laguito-no-2`
Cuban galera size of 152 mm by 38, marketed by Cohiba as Coronas Especiales. It is the shorter companion to the Laguito No. 1.

### Laguito No. 3
` /lah-GEE-toh/ | Vitolas & Shapes` — `laguito-no-3`
Cuban galera size of 115 mm by 26, sold as Cohiba Panetelas. It is the thinnest format in regular Habanos production.

### Lancero
` /lahn-SEH-roh/ | Vitolas & Shapes` — `lancero`
A slim parejo of roughly 7 1/2 inches by 38 to 40, usually finished with a flag or pigtail cap. Its Cuban galera name is Laguito No. 1, 192 mm by 38, created at El Laguito for Cohiba.

### Lonsdale
`Vitolas & Shapes` — `lonsdale`
A long, slim parejo, typically 6 1/2 to 6 3/4 inches by 42 or 43, named for the fifth Earl of Lonsdale. Cuban equivalents include Cervantes at 165 mm by 42 and Dalias at 170 mm by 43.

### Mareva
` /mah-REH-vah/ | Vitolas & Shapes` — `mareva`
The Cuban galera name for the petit corona, 129 mm by 42. More cigars have been rolled to this size than to any other in Habanos history.

`Vitolas & Shapes` — `nub`
A trademarked short, wide format from Oliva, generally 3 3/4 to 4 inches by 54 to 66. Its premise is that a cigar reaches its best burning zone after a short distance, so the format starts there and ends soon after.

### Panetela
` /pah-neh-TEH-lah/ | Vitolas & Shapes` — `panetela`
A slender parejo, generally 5 to 7 1/2 inches by 34 to 38 ring gauge. English-language catalogs often spell it panatela.

A shortened corona, roughly 5 to 5 1/8 inches by 40 to 42 ring gauge. Cuba's galera name for it is Mareva, 129 mm by 42, the most heavily produced size in Habanos history.

### Petit Robusto
` /peh-TEE roh-BOOS-toh/ | Vitolas & Shapes` — `petit-robusto`

` /proh-mee-NEN-teh/ | Vitolas & Shapes` — `prominente`
The Cuban galera name for the double corona, 194 mm by 49. Partagas Lusitanias and Hoyo de Monterrey Double Coronas are the best-known cigars made in it.

` /roh-BOOS-toh/ | Vitolas & Shapes` — `robusto-extra`

An older name for a short, thick cigar of roughly 4 1/2 to 5 inches by 48 to 50, said to have been commissioned by the Rothschild banking family in the nineteenth century. The word robusto has largely displaced it.

` /sah-loh-MOHN/ | Vitolas & Shapes` — `salomon`
A large perfecto with a tapered closed foot, a bulging midsection, and a pointed head, standardized in Cuba at 175 mm by 57. It ranks among the hardest shapes to bunch consistently.

` /roh-BOOS-toh/ | Vitolas & Shapes` — `short-robusto`

Shop shorthand for any cigar of 60 ring gauge, most often a 6 by 60 format. The word turns up on price lists and shelf tags far more than on bands.

` /pah-neh-TEH-lah/ | Vitolas & Shapes` — `slim-panetela`
A panetela at 30 ring gauge or below, commonly 4 1/2 to 6 inches long. Cohiba's Laguito No. 3, 115 mm by 26, is the best-known Cuban example.
